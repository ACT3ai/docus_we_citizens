# STOP School Violence Act (2018) — Student Surveillance Programs

* **Law:** STOP School Violence Act of 2018 (Students, Teachers, and Officers Preventing School Violence Act)
* **Passed:** House near-unanimous; Senate by unanimous consent — no recorded opposition vote
* **Signed by:** President Donald Trump, March 28, 2018
* **Funding:** $50 million over 10 years for threat-assessment programs and anonymous tip systems

Fourteen days after the Parkland school shooting, Congress passed a law funding the surveillance of children. No debate. No recorded vote in the Senate. Unanimous consent while parents were still burying their dead, while the political pressure to "do something" was at maximum. What Congress did was build a system that monitors your child's social media posts around the clock, invites anonymous accusations against students, puts teachers in the role of informants, and feeds more children into law enforcement contact — with no evidence that any of it prevents school shootings.

## How It Passed

The Parkland shooting occurred on February 14, 2018. Seventeen people were killed at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School in Parkland, Florida. Congress moved immediately. Not on the gun policy changes the Parkland survivors were demanding — those were blocked. What passed instead, with breathtaking speed and zero opposition, was a bill funding surveillance of the students themselves.

The STOP School Violence Act passed the House on an overwhelming bipartisan vote. In the Senate, it passed by unanimous consent — meaning no senator objected, no senator demanded a recorded vote, and no senator's vote is individually documented. Unanimous consent is the Senate's mechanism for passing uncontroversial matters quickly. It is used for naming post offices. It was used to create a $50 million student surveillance infrastructure.

The bill was signed by President Trump on March 28, 2018 — two weeks after the shooting. This timeline tells you everything about the quality of deliberation. There was no independent analysis of whether threat-assessment programs reduce school violence. There was no hearing on the documented harms of student surveillance. There was no consideration of how anonymous reporting systems could be weaponized. The political logic was simple: something must be passed. This was ready. Done.

The survivors of Parkland were demanding gun reforms. They got a law that would surveil millions of students nationwide. Their demand for action became the political cover for passing something entirely different. That is a specific kind of political manipulation — using the momentum of grief and outrage to pass legislation that was already waiting for the right crisis.

## What It Does To You

The STOP School Violence Act directed $50 million over ten years through the Comprehensive School Safety Initiative, administered by the Department of Justice's Bureau of Justice Assistance. The money was available for:

* Anonymous reporting applications, hotlines, and websites — systems where anyone (student, parent, community member, or rival) can file an anonymous tip alleging that a specific student poses a potential threat
* "Threat assessment" training and infrastructure — programs in which teachers, counselors, and administrators complete behavioral checklists on students who have been identified as potentially concerning, creating formal threat profiles on children
* Increased law enforcement presence in schools — School Resource Officers (SROs), who are law enforcement officers permanently stationed inside schools and who have arrest authority over students
* Social media monitoring contracts with private companies

**The social media monitoring component is the most invasive.** Companies including Social Sentinel, Gaggle, Bark for Schools, and similar vendors were funded and contracted to monitor students' social media activity — not just on school devices during school hours, but on personal devices, personal accounts, outside school hours, on weekends, over summer vacation. These systems scan for keywords, phrases, and images that algorithms flag as potentially indicating violence, self-harm, drug use, or other concerning behavior.

Students who post about being angry, post dark humor, quote song lyrics with violent themes, or discuss news events involving violence may be flagged by these systems. The flag goes to school administrators, who then decide whether to escalate to a threat assessment, contact parents, or involve law enforcement. The student often does not know their posts were flagged. The standard for triggering a flag is algorithmic, opaque, and applied without any individualized suspicion.

**The teacher-as-informant structure** requires educators to report students they find concerning based on behavioral checklists. Teachers fill out forms rating students on indicators like social isolation, "grievance narratives," unusual interest in past school shootings, and similar factors. The checklists are designed for well-intentioned threat assessment. In practice, they transform the teacher-student relationship into a supervised reporting relationship. A student who confides in a teacher, expresses strong emotions, or shows unusual interests may find themselves in a threat assessment process they did not know was happening.

**The anonymous tip trap.** Anonymous reporting systems like Sandy Hook Promise's "Say Something" app and similar platforms allow anyone to file an anonymous tip about a student with no accountability for false reports. A student who is bullied by classmates may find their bullies using the anonymous tip system as a weapon — filing fake "threats" attributed to the victim. The anonymity that was supposed to protect reporters from retaliation equally protects false accusers from any consequence. There is no mechanism in any of the funded systems for investigating the good faith of the reporter. Tips are investigated. Tippers are not.

## Rights It Strips

**First Amendment — Youth Free Speech**

The surveillance of students' social media accounts for concerning content directly chills youth speech. A teenager who knows that their posts are monitored by an algorithm looking for threatening or violent content, and that a flag could result in a threat assessment, law enforcement contact, or disciplinary action, will self-censor. Dark humor — a normal feature of adolescent communication — becomes a liability. Expressing anger about school, about peers, about society becomes a documented risk. The surveillance apparatus does not have to find anything to suppress speech. Its existence is sufficient.

The Supreme Court in Tinker v. Des Moines (1969) established that students do not "shed their constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate." The STOP School Violence Act's funding of systems that monitor student speech outside school hours, on personal devices, outside the schoolhouse gate entirely, extends the government-funded surveillance apparatus into spaces where students have the strongest expectation of autonomous speech.

**Fourth Amendment — Student Privacy**

Social media monitoring of personal accounts on personal devices outside school hours does not require individualized suspicion or any legal process under existing law. STOP Act-funded monitoring systems scan the communications of millions of students wholesale, flagging content for human review. This is mass surveillance of children — conducted without warrants, without individualized suspicion, and without meaningful opportunity for students or parents to contest the monitoring.

**Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment — Due Process**

Students who are subjected to threat assessment processes based on social media flags or anonymous tips may face significant consequences — suspension, mandatory counseling, law enforcement referrals, placement in alternative programs — without any formal hearing, without the ability to confront their accuser (who may be anonymous), and without access to the evidence against them. Threat assessment is administrative, not judicial — which means the procedural protections that apply in criminal or disciplinary proceedings do not automatically apply.

**The Right to Education Without Criminal Justice Contact**

Increased law enforcement presence in schools — another component of STOP Act funding — has a documented correlation with increased student arrests for ordinary school behavior. When a School Resource Officer is present for a classroom disruption that would previously have been handled by a teacher, the likelihood of that disruption resulting in arrest or charges increases dramatically. Funding SROs as a school safety measure directly increases students' risk of contact with the criminal justice system.

## Documented Harms

**Racial Disparities in Threat Assessment Are Documented**

Multiple studies of threat assessment programs found that Black and Latino students are referred to threat assessment at significantly higher rates than white students for the same behavior. A 2020 review of data from Virginia schools — which have had extensive threat assessment programs — found racial disparities in referral rates that could not be explained by behavioral differences. Threat assessment systems, however well-intentioned, import the implicit biases of the teachers and administrators who make referrals. When the result of a referral can include law enforcement contact, those racial disparities translate directly into disparate criminal justice consequences.

**School-to-Prison Pipeline Acceleration**

The National Council on Crime and Delinquency, the ACLU, and the Brennan Center for Justice have all documented that increased law enforcement presence in schools (SROs) correlates with increased arrests, charges, and court referrals of students for behavior that was previously handled by school discipline. Students who are arrested at school are statistically far more likely to drop out, far more likely to be arrested again, and far less likely to complete education. The STOP Act's funding of SROs is documented participation in the school-to-prison pipeline.

**ACLU Documentation of Student Social Media Surveillance**

The ACLU characterized school social media monitoring as a "grievous invasion of privacy" in a 2020 report on school surveillance technology. The report documented that students — including middle school students — had their personal accounts monitored without their knowledge, that the monitoring extended to after-hours personal communications, and that the flagging systems had high false-positive rates that resulted in students being subjected to threat assessment for posts that were clearly innocuous. The report found that students of color were disproportionately affected.

**Anonymous Tip System Abuse: Documented Cases**

Reporting on "Say Something" apps and similar anonymous tip systems has documented cases in which students used the systems to file false reports against classmates — as retaliation, as bullying, or as pranks. Students who were falsely reported faced threat assessments, police contact, and in some cases removal from school pending investigation. The students who made false reports faced no consequences because they were anonymous. The falsely accused students faced significant disruption to their education and, in some cases, lasting documentation in school records.

**Zero Documented Evidence of Effectiveness**

The most important fact about the STOP School Violence Act is that the programs it funds have not been demonstrated to prevent school shootings. There is no peer-reviewed research establishing that threat assessment programs reduce school shooting incidents. There is no evidence base supporting the claim that anonymous tip systems prevent mass violence events. The Parkland shooter was actually known to the FBI and local law enforcement before the attack — not because a tip system failed to identify him, but because existing law enforcement mechanisms failed to act on information they already had. The STOP Act's response to that failure was to fund surveillance of millions of other students — children who showed no signs of planning violence — while the systemic failure of response protocols went unaddressed.

**Technology Vendor Problems**

Social Sentinel, one of the major vendors receiving school social media monitoring contracts, was acquired and restructured in 2020 amid concerns about its flagging accuracy and data practices. Gaggle, another major vendor, stores flagged content from student communications in proprietary databases. Neither students nor parents have clear rights to access, correct, or delete data held by these vendors. The infrastructure created by STOP Act-funded contracts stores behavioral surveillance data on children with minimal data rights protections.

## Who Pushed This

**Representative John Rutherford (R-FL)** was the lead House sponsor. Rutherford is a former sheriff. His background is law enforcement. His solution to school violence was more surveillance and more law enforcement presence in schools — the tools of his prior career, applied to children.

**Speaker Paul Ryan (R-WI)** fast-tracked the bill through the House. Ryan needed to demonstrate that Congress was doing something in the immediate aftermath of Parkland. The STOP Act was available. It faced no organized opposition. It could be passed immediately. That it might cause significant harm to millions of students through surveillance and accelerated school-to-prison pipeline contact was not a primary consideration.

**The School Safety Technology Industry:** The passage of the STOP School Violence Act created a new revenue stream for school surveillance technology vendors. Companies selling social media monitoring, threat assessment software, anonymous reporting apps, and SRO technology services were the primary commercial beneficiaries. These companies have subsequently lobbied for expanded school surveillance funding at both the federal and state level. The industry that benefits from surveilling children advocated for the laws requiring it, funded implementation, and lobbied for its expansion. This is not a conspiracy theory — it is a documented commercial interest structure.

**Sandy Hook Promise** — a gun violence prevention nonprofit founded by parents of Parkland victims — supported the law and has promoted its anonymous tip programs. Sandy Hook Promise has sincere origins and motivations. Their support does not change the documented outcomes of the programs they advocate for.

**The NRA's Political Calculation.** The NRA supported the STOP School Violence Act as an alternative to gun legislation in the aftermath of Parkland. Funding school surveillance was acceptable to the NRA. Red-flag laws, enhanced background checks, and assault weapons regulations were not. The political function of the STOP Act was partly to provide cover for the refusal to pass the gun legislation the Parkland survivors actually demanded.

## Key Votes

**House:** Near-unanimous — the specific vote margin has been documented as overwhelming bipartisan support. There are no individual House members with a notable NO vote on record as a clean objection to the law's content.

**Senate:** Unanimous consent. No recorded vote. No senator is individually on record voting YES or NO. Every senator who was present and did not object to the unanimous consent request bears responsibility — and none of them is on the record for having voted.

Unanimous consent is a mechanism that makes every senator equally responsible for the outcome while making no single senator individually accountable. It is the perfect political tool for passing something that most senators do not want to answer for individually, while allowing the bill to pass quickly in a high-pressure political environment.

**President Trump** signed the bill March 28, 2018, describing it as a "very important bill" that would "make schools safer."

## Why This Matters for We The Citizens

The STOP School Violence Act represents a particular kind of government manipulation — the use of tragedy and emotional pressure to pass legislation that serves entrenched interests (the school safety technology industry, law enforcement agencies seeking expanded presence and authority) while harming the population it claims to protect (students, disproportionately students of color).

The children who attend schools with STOP Act-funded surveillance programs did not consent to being monitored. Their parents, in most cases, did not meaningfully consent. The communities most harmed by school-to-prison pipeline dynamics — predominantly Black and Latino communities — did not request more law enforcement in their schools.

A government that surveils its children teaches those children one thing above all else: you are presumed dangerous. Your communications are monitored. Your teachers report on you. Anonymous accusation can disrupt your life. This is not the lesson a free society should be teaching the next generation.

When a politician tells you they support school safety, ask them whether they support the evidence-based programs — improved mental health resources, trauma-informed teaching, restorative justice practices — that actually show results. Or whether they support the surveillance industry's preferred product: a system that monitors children, produces racial disparities, accelerates school-to-prison dynamics, and has never been shown to prevent a single school shooting.

The STOP Act is also a stark example of the rushed-legislation trap: genuine grief and a real problem (school violence) weaponized to pass pre-designed solutions that serve other interests. Every time you see Congress moving at emergency speed after a tragedy, ask who benefits from what's in the bill — and whether it actually addresses the tragedy or merely uses it.

# FOSTA-SESTA

* **Law:** Allow States and Victims to Fight Online Sex Trafficking Act / Stop Enabling Sex Traffickers Act (Public Law 115-164)
* **Passed:** April 2018
* **Vote:** Senate 97–2; House 388–25
* **Signed by:** President Trump

Congress killed a fundamental internet freedom protection with a 97–2 vote, wrapped it in the language of saving children, and then watched it fail to stop a single trafficking ring while stripping vulnerable people of the tools that kept them alive. FOSTA-SESTA is the template for how Congress destroys your rights: pick a sympathetic victim, write a vague law, vote yes, and dare anyone to object.

Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act was what made the open internet possible. It said platforms are not legally responsible for what their users post. Without it, every forum, comment section, and social network either shuts down or hires armies of lawyers to pre-screen every word you write. FOSTA-SESTA blew a hole in Section 230 by creating platform liability for content that "facilitates" sex trafficking — a term so broad and so undefined that platforms had no choice but to delete everything that could theoretically touch it.

The result was not fewer trafficking victims. The result was mass censorship of legal speech, the destruction of harm-reduction communities, and documented increases in violence against the most vulnerable people the law claimed to protect. Congress knew this would happen. Experts testified it would happen. Congress voted yes anyway — 97 to 2.

## How It Passed

In early 2018, the combined FOSTA and SESTA bills were moved through Congress with extraordinary speed and nearly zero opposition. The public framing was simple: platforms like Backpage were being used by sex traffickers, and victims needed to be able to sue. Who votes against protecting trafficking victims?

The Electronic Frontier Foundation, the American Civil Liberties Union, public health organizations, and sex worker advocacy groups all testified against the bills. They explained in plain language what would happen: platforms would not be able to distinguish legal adult content from trafficking-related content, so they would delete all of it. They explained that the people who would be hurt first and worst were not traffickers — it was the most vulnerable workers, who relied on online platforms to screen clients, share safety information, and work indoors rather than on the streets.

Congress did not care. The vote was 388–25 in the House and 97–2 in the Senate. Those numbers are not a sign of thoughtful consensus. They are a sign of a legislature that was more afraid of a negative campaign ad than it was interested in evidence. Anyone who voted yes and later claimed they did not anticipate the consequences either did not read the testimony or is lying.

## What It Does To You

FOSTA-SESTA does three things that together gut internet free speech:

First, it removes Section 230 immunity for platforms when content "facilitates" sex trafficking. The word "facilitates" is not defined in any meaningful way. Platforms cannot know in advance whether a court will decide their content met that standard, so they delete anything that could arguably come close.

Second, it creates a new federal crime for operating a platform "with the intent to promote or facilitate prostitution." Again: vague. Prosecutors have wide discretion. Platforms have every incentive to over-comply.

Third, it opens platforms to civil lawsuits from alleged victims. Civil suits do not require the high evidentiary standard of criminal prosecution. One lawsuit, even a losing one, can cost a small platform its existence.

The practical result: the moment FOSTA-SESTA passed, Craigslist shut down its Personals section entirely — a section used by millions of adults for completely legal activities. Hundreds of other platforms followed. Forums, harm-reduction communities, LGBTQ+ spaces, and adult entertainment communities that had existed for years were deleted within days. Not because they were trafficking. Because the risk of being accused of facilitating something that could be called trafficking was too high to accept.

## Rights It Strips

**Your right to speak on open platforms.** Section 230 is what makes platforms feel safe enough to host your words. Once you establish that Congress can create exceptions to Section 230 immunity, every subsequent Congress has the power to expand those exceptions. FOSTA-SESTA did not just harm sex workers — it established the legal framework for future censorship by liability.

**Your right to access information.** Harm-reduction communities that shared safety information, client screening tools — what sex workers called "bad date lists" of dangerous men — and health resources were taken down overnight. People who needed that information to stay alive no longer had it.

**Your right to organize online.** Entire communities — LGBTQ+, adult content creators, harm-reduction networks — lost their gathering spaces. Not because a court found them guilty of anything. Because a platform had to choose between keeping them and facing open-ended legal liability.

**The right to due process for platforms.** FOSTA-SESTA creates liability even without intent or knowledge. A platform can be sued for content it never saw, did not create, and had no reason to expect. That is not a legal standard — it is an invitation to preemptive mass censorship.

## Documented Abuses

The data is not ambiguous:

A 2019 study by researchers at Tufts University found that **36% of sex workers reported increased violence** after FOSTA-SESTA passed. **72% reported increased economic instability.** These are not abstract statistics — these are people who lost their safety tools and were pushed into more dangerous conditions because Congress wanted a good vote.

The digital safety infrastructure that FOSTA-SESTA destroyed was specific and documented. "Bad date lists" — shared databases of dangerous clients — were taken down immediately when platforms shut down. Client verification tools disappeared. Indoor work became harder to find, forcing street-based work where violence is more common and law enforcement less useful.

The June 2021 Government Accountability Office report delivered the most damning indictment: in the three years after FOSTA-SESTA passed, the Department of Justice used the new criminal penalties it created **exactly once.** One prosecution. Three years. Meanwhile, documented harm to thousands of vulnerable people piled up.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation documented a wave of over-censorship that swept platforms immediately after passage. Entire communities — not just sex-work-adjacent content but LGBTQ+ health communities, dating apps, harm-reduction forums — deleted themselves or were deleted by platforms afraid of undefined liability. Legal speech, protected by the First Amendment, gone because Congress could not be bothered to write a law that actually targeted the problem it claimed to address.

The law was sold as a tool to fight trafficking. The DOJ used it once in three years. Backpage — the primary target — was seized through a separate federal enforcement action that did not require FOSTA-SESTA to exist. The law was not needed to stop Backpage. It was needed to give Congress a talking point and to establish the precedent that Section 230 can be gutted whenever the political cover is good enough.

## Who Pushed This

The primary sponsors were Senator Rob Portman (R-OH) and Senator Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) in the Senate, and Representative Ann Wagner (R-MO) and Representative Walberg (R-MI) in the House. The bills had the backing of nearly every child-protection and anti-trafficking organization with a public profile — legitimate organizations with legitimate concerns who were sold a law that did not deliver what they wanted.

Behind them was the broader coalition of lawmakers who had been looking for years for a way to crack Section 230. Tech platform liability is a bipartisan goal: Republicans want to punish platforms they see as biased against conservatives; Democrats want to hold platforms accountable for misinformation and harm. FOSTA-SESTA gave both sides a cover story — fighting trafficking — to take the first bite out of Section 230 protection.

The telecommunications industry, which competes with tech platforms, supported the bill. So did legacy media companies, who have long wanted to see the Section 230 protections that enable competition with their business model weakened.

The voices that were not listened to: the EFF, the ACLU, public health researchers, sex worker advocacy organizations, and anyone who read the actual testimony.

## Key Votes

* **Senate: 97–2.** The two NO votes were Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR) — who co-authored Section 230 and understood exactly what was being destroyed — and Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY).
* **House: 388–25.** The 25 NO votes represent the entire pool of House members who refused the easy political cover and voted based on evidence.

Every member who voted YES and later claimed the harms were unexpected is not telling the truth. The harms were testified to, documented in advance, and predictable to anyone who read the bill. The YES votes were not mistakes. They were political choices.

## Why This Matters for We The Citizens

FOSTA-SESTA is the most important template in recent congressional history for how to destroy a constitutional right without ever saying you are doing that. The formula:

1. Pick a victim category that is emotionally unchallengeable (trafficking victims, children).
2. Write a law with vague liability standards that will produce mass over-compliance.
3. Hold the vote when the political cover is at maximum — no one wants to be the senator who voted "against" trafficking victims.
4. Watch platforms censor legal speech to avoid undefined liability.
5. When the harms are documented, claim you did not anticipate them.

This formula has been used repeatedly since 2018. A politician who cannot articulate why FOSTA-SESTA was bad policy — not just the good intentions but the actual outcome — is a politician who will vote for the next version of it. That vote is coming. It always comes.

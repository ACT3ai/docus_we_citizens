# What gets assessed

## The evidence

For somebody who has never held office, the record is what they have said and done in public:

* **Long-form interviews and appearances.** Hours, not clips. A person's answer to a follow-up
  question is worth more than any prepared statement, which is why length matters.
* **Their own writing** — books, articles, published positions.
* **Explicit commitments** — what they have said they would do, in terms specific enough to be checked
  against later.
* **Background** — what they have actually done: work, service, things built or run.

## How it is read

Material is assessed against the **published qualification rubrics** — sixteen areas, hundreds of
graded criteria, each with levels 1 to 10 written down *before* anybody was assessed against them.

The rubrics are deliberately written so that people who disagree about policy can both score well. A
market-oriented Democrat, an industrial-policy Republican and a deregulatory libertarian can each reach
a 9 in the same area by naming a **mechanism** and saying **who pays**. What loses points is not having
the wrong answer; it is having no mechanism.

## Percent known

Every assessment carries a companion figure: how much of what we would want to know we actually have.

This is the number that keeps the roster honest. A 90% assessment built on one appearance is not a
strong result, it is a weak sample, and the product refuses to present the two identically. A low
percent-known is an invitation — it says exactly what contribution would improve the record.

## What never feeds this

The voting record, if one exists. The wall runs in both directions: in-office acts feed Monkey and
Llama and never touch the Flamingo, and none of the material above ever touches Monkey or Llama.

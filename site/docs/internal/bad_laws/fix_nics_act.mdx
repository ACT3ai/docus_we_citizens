# Fix NICS Act (2018) — Expanding the Federal Gun Prohibition Database

* **Law:** Fix NICS Act of 2017/2018 — enacted as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018
* **Signed by:** President Donald Trump, March 23, 2018
* **Mechanism:** Embedded in an omnibus spending bill — no standalone floor vote on the gun policy changes

The federal government has a list. You cannot see your file in it. You cannot review the records that put you there. If your name is on it, you are denied the ability to buy a firearm — and the denial happens automatically, in seconds, before any court has reviewed your case or any judge has ruled on whether you are actually a danger to anyone. Approximately 168,000 veterans are on this list not because they committed a crime or because a court found them dangerous, but because the Department of Veterans Affairs decided they needed help managing their finances. The Fix NICS Act was passed to make this list bigger. It was buried in an omnibus spending bill so no member of Congress would have to cast a standalone vote defending it.

## How It Passed

The Fix NICS Act was introduced following the 2017 Sutherland Springs church shooting in Texas, in which the shooter had a disqualifying military conviction that the Air Force had never submitted to the National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS). The failure was real: existing law already prohibited the shooter from purchasing firearms, but the Air Force had not reported the conviction. The Fix NICS Act was drafted to prevent that specific failure.

The mechanism chosen to pass the law was significant. Rather than holding standalone floor votes in both chambers on gun policy — which would require members to cast an explicit record vote on expanding the firearms prohibition database — congressional leadership embedded the Fix NICS provisions inside the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018: a massive omnibus spending bill that funds the entire federal government. Voting against the omnibus meant voting against all government funding. No member voted specifically on the gun provisions. They voted on the spending bill.

Gun Owners of America warned at the time that the law's language went beyond fixing the specific reporting failure it claimed to address and instead created systemic pressure on every federal agency and federal court to maximize the number of records submitted to NICS — whether or not each record reflects an actual threat.

## What It Does To You

**The Requirement**

The Fix NICS Act requires every federal agency and every federal court to certify whether it has submitted all records of people who are prohibited from buying firearms to the NICS database. If an agency or court has not submitted records, it must create an implementation plan to begin doing so and to "maximize record submissions." The Act provided $125 million per year for fiscal years 2018–2022 for states to add records and $250 million per year for the National Criminal History Improvement Program (NCHIP) — the system used to compile state records into the federal database.

This is not a neutral technical fix. This is a structured, funded, multi-year campaign to find more records and add more people to the federal gun prohibition list. The goal of "maximizing" submissions means the default posture of every agency is: find more records, report more people.

**Veterans: Prohibited for Needing Help With Finances**

The most documented harm from the existing NICS database — made larger by the Fix NICS Act — is what happened to veterans. When the Department of Veterans Affairs determines that a veteran is unable to manage their VA benefits and appoints a fiduciary to manage those benefits on their behalf, the VA reports that determination to NICS. The veteran is added to the gun prohibition database.

This is not a criminal conviction. It is not a court order finding the veteran is a danger to themselves or others. It is an administrative determination — often made based on age-related cognitive decline, physical disability, or mental health conditions that impair financial management — that has nothing inherently to do with firearms.

Approximately 168,000 veterans were added to the NICS prohibited list through this VA reporting mechanism as of recent years. Many of them had no idea they were on the list until they tried to buy a firearm and were denied. They had served their country, returned home, and found that seeking help from the VA — the agency created to support them — had cost them a constitutional right.

**The False Match Problem**

NICS operates by matching a buyer's name, birthdate, and other identifying information against the database. It cannot reliably distinguish between people who share a name. People with common surnames — disproportionately affecting Hispanic and Black Americans who share surnames — are denied at higher rates because similar names trigger false positives against entries in the database.

The FBI's NICS Operations Center has processes for challenging a false denial, but those processes require you to affirmatively challenge the denial and provide documentation that you are not the person in the database. You have three business days to resolve the denial before the sale is automatically prohibited. Three business days to prove you are not someone else.

## Rights It Strips

**Second Amendment — Permanent Administrative Prohibition Without Conviction**

The Second Amendment protects the right to keep and bear arms. NICS-based prohibition has been upheld for people with criminal convictions and civil commitment orders. But the Fix NICS Act expands the database with records that do not reflect criminal convictions or formal judicial proceedings — they reflect administrative determinations by bureaucracies. A VA fiduciary appointment is not a criminal conviction. A financial management issue is not evidence of dangerousness. Yet the result is the same: permanent prohibition from purchasing firearms, enforced automatically.

**Due Process — You Cannot Access Your Own File**

When a gun buyer is denied by NICS, they cannot access the records in the NICS database that triggered the denial. The buyer cannot see what specific record caused the flag. They cannot review the accuracy of the underlying record. They cannot verify whether they are the actual person the record pertains to or a false match. The system that can permanently deny a constitutional right operates with structural opacity toward the person it denies.

The NICS Privacy Act exemptions allow the government to withhold your NICS records from you, citing law enforcement and national security. This means the government can add records to a database that permanently affects your constitutional rights, and you may not know what was added, why it was added, or how to contest it.

**Equal Protection — Disparate Impact by Name**

The false positive problem is not uniformly distributed. Name-matching systems that generate false denials hit people with common surnames disproportionately. Researchers and advocacy organizations have documented that Black and Hispanic Americans experience false denial rates at higher proportions than white Americans with less common surnames. The government knows this. The Fix NICS Act did not include any requirement to improve the accuracy of name-based matching before expanding the database.

## Documented Harms

**168,000 Veterans Stripped of Rights Without Due Process**

The veteran fiduciary prohibition is the most thoroughly documented harm in the NICS system. The National Rifle Association, Gun Owners of America, and veterans' rights organizations have documented thousands of individual cases. Veterans discovered their prohibited status when denied at gun counters. They had no prior notice, no hearing, no opportunity to contest the determination before the prohibition was imposed.

Congress subsequently passed the Veterans 2nd Amendment Protection Act to provide some relief — requiring a judicial finding that a veteran is a danger before the VA can report them to NICS. But implementation has been inconsistent, and hundreds of thousands of veterans remain in the database from prior reports under the old rules.

**5.1 Million Denials, Almost Zero Prosecutions**

Since NICS began operation in 1998, over 5.1 million denials have been issued. Lying on the Federal Firearms Transaction Record (ATF Form 4473) when attempting to buy a gun while prohibited is a federal felony — 10 years in prison. Despite 5.1 million denials, the Department of Justice prosecuted fewer than 100 individuals per year for this offense in most recent years. The government denies millions of people, presumes them to be prohibited persons, and then almost never prosecutes the cases it just flagged as federal felonies.

This either means: (a) most of the denials are false positives — people who are not actually prohibited being denied, or (b) the government is not serious about the underlying enforcement rationale. Either interpretation is a condemnation of the system the Fix NICS Act was designed to expand.

**Omnibus Process Hid the Vote**

No member of Congress cast a standalone vote on the Fix NICS provisions. The law that expanded the federal firearms prohibition database and funded a decade-long campaign to maximize the number of records submitted to it passed as a line item in a must-pass omnibus spending bill. This is how major changes to constitutional rights get enacted with no accountability: attach them to something no one can vote against.

## Who Pushed This

The Fix NICS Act was championed bipartisanly. In the Senate, the lead sponsors were Senator John Cornyn (R-TX) and Senator Chris Murphy (D-CT). In the House, the bill was sponsored by Representative John Culberson (R-TX) and Representative Elizabeth Egan (R-PA), among others.

Gun Owners of America opposed the bill: "Fix NICS Bill Would Help Block Gun Sales to Peaceful People." The GOA warned that the law would sweep in people who are not dangerous, expand the prohibition database based on administrative records lacking due process, and create financial incentives for states and agencies to maximize submissions rather than accurate submissions.

The National Rifle Association supported the bill, a position that created significant controversy within gun rights circles.

## Key Votes

The Fix NICS Act had no standalone floor vote in either chamber. It was enacted as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018.

**Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018**
* House: 256–167 (March 22, 2018)
* Senate: 65–32 (March 23, 2018)

Members who voted YES voted for the omnibus and therefore enacted the Fix NICS provisions, whether or not they intended to or focused on those provisions. Members who voted NO did not vote specifically against Fix NICS — they voted against the entire funding package.

This ambiguity is the point. Omnibus bills protect members from accountability for individual provisions. No one is ever on record voting specifically to expand the gun prohibition database.

## Why This Matters for We The Citizens

168,000 veterans lost a constitutional right because they asked the VA for help. They were not convicted. They were not found dangerous. They were not given a hearing. They received a letter saying their benefits would be managed by a fiduciary, and somewhere in the federal bureaucracy, a record was sent to NICS, and the next time they walked into a gun store, they were denied.

That is what "fix" looks like when the government does it. Expand the list. Fund more submissions. Maximize the number of people prohibited. Do it inside a spending bill so no one has to defend it in public.

The senators and representatives who embedded this in an omnibus are on record voting for it. The senators who voted NO on the omnibus voted against it — but they were voting against the whole bill, not specifically these provisions. There is no clean vote record on Fix NICS because there was no standalone vote. That omission is itself the story.

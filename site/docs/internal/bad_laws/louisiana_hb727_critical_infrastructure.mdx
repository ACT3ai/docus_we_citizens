# Louisiana HB 727 — Critical Infrastructure Law (2018)

* **Law:** Louisiana House Bill 727, amending Louisiana Revised Statutes 14:61 (criminal trespass)
* **Passed:** May 2018
* **Vote:** Louisiana House 88–5; Senate 38–0 (unanimous in Senate)
* **Signed by:** Governor John Bel Edwards (Democrat)
* **Modeled on:** Oklahoma HB 1123 (2017)

## What It Does

HB 727 follows the Oklahoma template and heightens criminal penalties for activity near Louisiana's extensive oil, gas, and pipeline infrastructure:

* **Enhanced felony penalties** — Trespass on or near critical infrastructure (pipelines, refineries, LNG terminals, chemical plants) is classified as a felony with up to 5 years imprisonment and fines up to $1,000.
* **Organizational liability** — Persons who "conspire" to commit critical infrastructure trespass face enhanced penalties; organizations that fund or support such activity face significant fines.
* **Broad infrastructure definition** — Louisiana's definition covers the extensive petrochemical corridor along the Mississippi River (often called "Cancer Alley") as well as offshore platforms and terminal facilities.

## Why Critics Oppose It

* **Targets environmental and climate activists** — Louisiana's oil and gas infrastructure runs through communities with documented environmental justice concerns. The law's practical effect is to criminalize protests by residents and activists who oppose pipeline construction, chemical plant expansion, and related projects.
* **Signed by a Democrat** — Governor Edwards's signature demonstrates that these laws have bipartisan political cover; opposition to fossil fuel protest legislation is not simply a partisan issue.
* **Criminalizes proximity near communities** — Unlike remote pipeline rights-of-way in Oklahoma or North Dakota, much of Louisiana's critical infrastructure runs through or adjacent to residential neighborhoods in communities like St. James and St. John the Baptist parishes. The "critical infrastructure" designation covers areas where residents live.
* **Cancer Alley context** — Environmental justice advocates in Cancer Alley have used protest and direct action as primary tools because standard regulatory channels have repeatedly failed. Felony protest laws remove one of their last effective levers.
* **Industry lobbying** — As with Oklahoma, the American Fuel and Petrochemical Manufacturers (AFPM) and related industry groups lobbied for the bill.

## Constitutional Concerns

* 1st Amendment (assembly, petition) — felony elevation for protest near infrastructure chills protected assembly in communities where the infrastructure is literally next door.
* 14th Amendment (equal protection / environmental justice) — the law disproportionately affects Black and low-income communities in Cancer Alley who lack alternative forums to raise environmental grievances.
* 1st Amendment (organizational association) — conspiracy provisions that penalize funding or supporting protest activity.

## Part of a Pattern

Louisiana was the second major state to pass a critical infrastructure protection bill after Oklahoma. This law, combined with North Dakota SB 2044 (2019) and Texas HB 3557, forms a national template designed to shut down infrastructure protest. See [State Critical Infrastructure Laws](/docs/internal/bad-laws/state-critical-infrastructure-laws).

## Why This Matters for We The Citizens

Louisiana HB 727's unanimous Senate passage and Democratic gubernatorial signature shows that suppressing protest near energy infrastructure has bipartisan institutional support. A state legislator in Louisiana, or any state that copied this template, who voted YES is on record choosing industry access over citizens' right to protest next to their own homes.

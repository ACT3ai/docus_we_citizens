# The standard

An ethical standard nobody wrote down is a mood, and a mood cannot be violated in any checkable way.
This one is written down, in three places, and all three are public.

## 1. The founding documents

The plain-language promises in them: consent of the governed, equality before the law, a government
answerable to the people it governs. Whatever else is argued about them, those promises are on the
page and they are testable against how the country actually runs.

## 2. The laws Congress writes for everyone else

The clearest available statement of what Congress itself says is right. When a body writes a rule for
three hundred million people, it has stated a standard — and it has stated it far more precisely than
any speech does.

This makes one particular failure very easy to detect: **the body exempting itself from the rule it
just wrote.** No interpretation is needed. The rule exists, the exemption exists, and both are
published.

## 3. The social contract as citizens actually hold it

Not the version described in a civics textbook — the version people are actually operating on when
they say Washington does not work for them. That is the yardstick this site measures against, and it
is set down explicitly rather than assumed. See
**[The Social Contract](../social_contract/overview.mdx)**.

## When the three disagree, the disagreement is the finding

This is the useful part. Three written standards give you three ways to be wrong, and every gap
between them is a documented, sourced observation rather than an opinion:

* Founding promise says one thing, statute does another → a constitutional question, argued in
  public for two centuries and worth arguing again.
* Statute says one thing, the body exempts itself → the exemption is the whole finding, and it needs
  no further interpretation.
* Everything is formally in order and citizens do not believe a word of it → the gap is between the
  written contract and the lived one, which is the most important of the three and the one nobody
  measures.

## Misalignment is not the same as disagreement

A citizen who wants a different policy is disagreeing. A system that produces outcomes nobody
selected, that survives elections meant to change it, is **misaligned** — a different thing, with a
different remedy. The distinction is drawn in
**[Misalignment vs disagreement](../social_contract/misalignment_vs_disagreement.mdx)** and it runs
under everything in this area.

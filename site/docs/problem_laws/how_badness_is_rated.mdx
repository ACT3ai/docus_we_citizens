# How badness is rated

Each problem law carries a **badness rating from 1 to 100**. It is the weight the law brings into
every calculation downstream, so it is worth being precise about where the number comes from.

## What the number means

The rating answers one question: *how much of the damage to representative democracy is attributable
to this law?* A 95 is a law that closed a door the public cannot reopen. A 20 is a law that made
something worse at the margin.

It is **not** a measure of how angry the law makes people, how much coverage it got, or how recent it
is. An old law that is still doing the damage rates higher than a new one that made headlines.

## Who sets it, and what citizens move

* The rating is **proposed with the record** and carries its reasoning in the file.
* **Citizens vote on it.** The vote is real and it is public.
* The vote **orders the list** — it decides what a citizen sees first, and what the movement argues
  about next.

That distinction is deliberate. Letting a popular vote directly set the multiplier on a number we
publish about named living people would make the arithmetic a popularity contest, and a popularity
contest is exactly the thing this movement exists to replace.

## The vehicle problem

Damaging provisions are often buried inside bills nobody could vote against — a defense
authorisation, an appropriations package, a must-pass continuing resolution. The record captures
**which vehicle carried the provision** as context, and it stays context: it is shown to the reader
and it does not enter the arithmetic. A citizen deserves to know that a representative's yes vote was
a yes on a 4,000-page package. They also deserve to know the provision passed.

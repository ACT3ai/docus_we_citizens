# Your Profile, Your Anonymity

Your profile is what another citizen sees attached to your work — a decision you recorded, an evidence chain you built, a fix you proposed. You decide whether that carries your real name or a pseudonym, and you can change your mind at any moment. This exists because a citizen who documents what an official actually did has a rational reason to fear being punished for it, and being able to disappear from public view is part of being able to do the work at all.

## What it does

Your account carries three separate name-shaped fields and one setting that decides which of them the app renders. There is a **handle**, the public address other people link to. There is a **pseudonym**, issued automatically when you sign up — three ordinary nouns drawn from a screened, politically neutral word list and checked for uniqueness, so a brand-new account has a working pseudonym before it has anything else. And there is your **real name**, which is stored but not shown unless you say to show it. The setting resolves to anonymous, real, or both. Anonymous is the default, and publishing your real name takes a deliberate act.

Every read of a profile is assembled on the server into exactly one of three shapes: what any other citizen gets, what you get for your own profile, and what an admin on that install gets. Each shape is **built from what the reader is allowed to see**, rather than a full record with the private fields deleted afterwards. That difference matters: a forgotten deletion is a leak that nobody notices, while a forgotten addition is a missing field somebody reports. An email address is admin-only unconditionally — there is no setting, opt-in, or request that publishes one.

The profile then shows what you have actually published, not a summary of it. Decisions, value systems, proposed laws, evidence chains, submissions into the review queue, and comments you wrote in review threads each get a panel with your most recent entries as live links straight to the record, plus a full list behind them. The counts come from the same stores the links open, filtered through the same projection, so what you see on the page is what exists. Beside them sit a trust panel and a karma panel, each linking through to the arithmetic that produced it.

A separate control governs whether you appear publicly at all, and it runs four steps: **listed**, **unlisted** (no roster row, but your link still works), **private**, and **hidden**. Private is the safety valve. You stay counted in the community, but the roster card becomes a stable **citizen number** — a random draw from a wide range, assigned the first time you go private and kept forever, so switching privacy off and on again never produces a fresh number an observer could correlate with the name that just vanished. The direct link to your profile answers exactly as if that handle had never existed. The switch takes effect immediately, needs no reason, no approval and no waiting period, and it never touches your trust level or how much your input weighs anywhere in the product.

## The goal it serves

A citizen who publishes a sourced account of how an official voted is doing something a powerful person may want punished. The fear of an employer, an agency, or a mob is not paranoia, and a product that treats it as an edge case will be used only by people with nothing to lose. Pseudonymity here is a safety feature, not a convenience toggle. A system that quietly penalized people for using it would push exactly the citizens most at risk into either danger or irrelevance, which is why nothing on the [trust ladder](/docs/trust_scores/overview) requires a legal name and a pseudonymous citizen can climb all the way to the top of it.

The argument this whole site makes is that [the record itself](/docs/open_data/overview) is the evidence, and that arithmetic over the public record is not an attack on anybody. If that is true, then the identity of the person doing the arithmetic is not load-bearing. Anyone can check the work; nobody needs to know who checked it first. Requiring a real name would add nothing to the proof and would subtract the contributors with the most to lose.

There is a second reason, and it is about what happens after the fear passes. Someone who becomes frightened and has to choose between deleting years of work and staying exposed will usually delete. So going private costs you nothing: not your standing, not your karma, not your place in a [community's](/docs/communities/overview) count. The exit is cheap on purpose, because a cheap exit is what makes it survivable to have been visible in the first place.

## What keeps it honest

The product states plainly what this cannot promise, and the same sentences appear in the app rather than only here.

* **An admin on your install can see your identity.** That is unavoidable if anyone is going to moderate impersonation or doxxing. The check on it is that every such lookup is written to an audit trail and shown back to you in your own account history, so unmasking is not silent.
* **A determined observer watching the roster over time may still correlate a disappearance with a new anonymized entry.** Anonymized cards deliberately carry no join month, no record counts, and no activity signal, but full unlinkability against someone snapshotting the roster daily is not achievable and the copy will not claim it.
* **Going private is forward-looking.** Anything you already published keeps the attribution it was published with. Retracting published records is a separate, explicit action, and the two are never merged into one button that promises more than it does.
* **Parts of this are specified and not yet built.** The anonymized roster card, the citizen number map behind it, and the `hidden` step are written down but not implemented; today a private citizen is simply absent from the roster rather than present as a number.

Switching to a pseudonym or going private is never a paid feature and never needs approval. Going anonymous is safety, and safety is not a product.

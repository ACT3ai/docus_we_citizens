# Videos, Transcripts And Provenance

A person asking for your vote for the first time has no roll call behind them. What they do have is hours of themselves talking: interviews, town halls, podcasts, speeches. Those hours are the richest checkable evidence about them that exists, and they are worth nothing while they are still a link. This is the machinery that turns a link into words a citizen can read, quote, and check against the tape.

## What it does

**The app never downloads media.** It does not fetch audio, it does not import a back catalogue, and it does not call a video host for a file — not under a flag, not with a checkbox ticked, not in any environment. That refusal is checked by an automated build guard that reads the source tree, string literals included, because a tool name passed as a string is still a call. Any executable reference to a fetcher fails the build.

That refusal is the entire reason the **citizen mining network** exists. A work queue of videos with no transcript is published as a plain file in a public repository, so it needs no account and no permission to work from — anybody may transcribe a public video. Volunteers opt in on their own computers, run the movement's published prompt on their own schedule, and publish the words plus a record of how they made them into a repository they own. Five hundred missing transcripts is an impossible bill for one server and a pleasant afternoon for thirty citizens.

Distribution is also what makes the result believable. A single central transcript is an unchecked claim, so independent copies of the same video are compared to each other: where a clear majority agree, the canonical copy is trusted; where only one copy exists, it is usable and flagged as uncorroborated; where the copies do not agree, nothing is trusted and the page says so in plain words — three citizens transcribed this video and they do not agree, so we are not quoting it until they do.

What the app holds is the result. The transcript text, unedited, because a transcript is a record and an edited one is poison to the whole comparison. The channel and video records that point back at the original. And **provenance** — a mark on the transcript file, never on the person, saying how the words were obtained. A caption track a human wrote is one thing. A platform's automatic captions, which mishear and separate no speakers, are another. A machine transcription carrying word timings and speaker turns is a third. When a quote rests on a weak one, the caveat renders beside it in its own space and never in a tooltip: *these words come from automatic captions, and speakers are not separated.*

One control renders those words everywhere they appear: a timecode gutter, a who-is-speaking key with the person the page is about in solid black, minute markers, and gaps drawn in position rather than silently skipped. Click any sentence and a wide button appears under the player reading **Move play head to 12:58**. It seeks without playing, so a citizen can line up the moment before watching it.

Each video also has its own page listing **every** qualification area, whether that video spoke to it or not. The empty rows are the point, and each blank carries a stated cause. *He did not talk about economics in this interview* and *nobody has read this video yet* are different facts about different things, and a reader who cannot tell them apart will read the second as the first.

## The goal it serves

The incumbent's structural advantage is that only the incumbent has a record. A citizen sizing up a challenger otherwise has campaign advertising, endorsements, and a party organization's say-so — which is exactly the terrain that money and organized influence already own. A two-hour interview is the one place where a person's reasoning becomes something you can weigh instead of something you have an impression about, and most of it is sitting in public, unread by anyone deciding how to vote.

This is what makes that material usable. It is the reason [Good New Leaders](/docs/new_politicians/overview) can be assessed on anything at all, and it is the evidence base under every Flamingo Award figure the movement publishes about somebody who has never held office. Without it, [replacing a captured incumbent](/docs/replace_incumbents/overview) means asking a party's base to get behind a stranger on faith, and faith is what the current arrangement already runs on.

The insistence on provenance is not fussiness. A machine mishears, and publishing a claim about a named living person out of a machine transcription without saying so is how a movement earns a lawsuit and deserves one. Stating how the words were obtained, every single time, lets a citizen price the uncertainty for themselves rather than being asked to trust us. It is the same discipline the [official record sourcing](/docs/open_data/the_sourcing_rule) rule applies to votes, carried over to speech.

## What keeps it honest

* **The refusal to fetch is enforced, not promised.** A build guard fails the build on any executable reference to a downloader, so it is a rule the software cannot quietly stop obeying between releases.
* **Weak provenance lowers stated confidence and renders a caveat. It never withholds a finding.** A rule that would have vetoed any finding resting on a weak transcript was proposed and rejected outright. The Flamingo Award's absence has two meanings, so quietly declining to assess somebody does not produce nothing — it publishes *unknown* about them, in public, beside people who were assessed. That is a different claim than the evidence supports.
* **Speaker separation is not trusted further than it was measured.** On noisy long-form recordings the machine over-splits badly: one man talking alone for over three hours came back as seventy-two separate voices. So the key collapses unresolved voices into one honest line rather than inventing a wall of names.
* **A guess is allowed; an undeclared guess is not.** Where the pipeline works out whose words it is reading, it publishes that it guessed, by what method, and how sure it is. Uncertainty about whether we heard *them* moves how much we claim to know about the person, and never the assessment itself.
* **Only the subject's own words count.** An interviewer's question, a co-host's monologue, or an inserted clip are not evidence about the subject. A person merely mentioned in somebody else's video is linked as mentioned and excluded from their own assessment.
* **The transcript is how you find the moment, not a replacement for it.** The citable source is the recording.

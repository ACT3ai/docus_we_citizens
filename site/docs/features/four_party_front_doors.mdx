# The Four Party Front Doors

Besides this site, We The Citizens runs four more: WeCitizensR.com for Republicans, WeCitizensD.com for Democrats, WeCitizensL.com for Libertarians, and WeCitizensSocialism.com for Democratic Socialists. They are not four organizations, four data sets, or four sets of numbers. They are one running program with one public record behind it, answering to four addresses, because a voter will look much harder at their own side's officeholders than at a site carrying no label they recognize.

## What it does

One web app serves all five addresses. When a request arrives, the program reads the domain name it came in on and resolves an **edition**: R, D, L, S, or the neutral main site. Nothing else in the program reads a raw hostname; every page asks which edition it is serving and gets an answer. An address the program does not recognize resolves to the neutral main site, never to some other audience's party page.

Each door then renders in its own voice, with its own opening page, accent colour, wording and set of content areas, written from its own messaging. **The four doors are independent, not mirrors of one another.** One may carry areas another does not, and one may be improved on its own without owing a matching change to the other three. Today the Republican and Libertarian doors carry twenty content areas and the Democrat and Democratic Socialist doors carry sixteen, because that is how those audiences' messaging is written.

What a door may change is chrome and wording. What it may never change is anything that could move a number:

* The award arithmetic, and every threshold, weight and formula inside it.
* Who is on a roster of officeholders or challengers.
* The evidence, the sources, and the roll-call rows behind them.
* The meaning of a red or green bar.

That is enforced by structure, not by a promise. An edition's committed configuration file has a fixed shape with nowhere to put a threshold, and a build check compares every edition's configuration against a reference edition and fails on any committed difference outside a short permitted list: the edition letter, the domain, the display name, the party label, the accent colour, the analytics tag, and the path to that door's section of this site. That check covers configuration only. It has nothing to say about how many content areas a door carries or what they argue. An incumbent at 71% of the Monkey Award renders exactly the same way on their own party's door as on anyone else's.

The party colour is confined to chrome. It tints a chip, a link and one button. **It never tints an award bar**, where red means adverse and green means good on every door regardless of which party that door serves. Party is shown as a letter after a person's name, never by colouring the name, the photograph, or a bar.

Every door names the other three, in the same fixed order, in its menu and its footer, with the door you are standing on marked rather than dropped from the list.

## The goal it serves

A committed partisan who lands on a neutral site reads it, within seconds, as *this is for the other team*. That is not a defect in the reader. It is what happens when somebody who arrived from a social-media argument thirty seconds ago is asked to reason about the social contract from first principles before being shown anything concrete.

Cross-party persuasion is expensive and slow. In-party accountability is cheap and fast, because the visitor already agrees with the party label and only has to be shown that *this particular incumbent* is not delivering on it. The doors lower the first ask to one sentence: here are your elected officials, here is what they voted for, here is who could replace them. Getting a party's own base behind one strong challenger before a primary, rather than splitting three ways after it, is the highest-leverage act available to an ordinary voter, and it is the strategy the movement is built around. [Replacing Captured Incumbents](/docs/replace_incumbents/overview) is that argument in full.

The seat panel is where a door either earns trust or loses it. Every seat shows three columns: the person currently in office, the strongest challenger from the visitor's own side, and the strongest challenger who is *not* from that side, whoever that turns out to be and whatever party they belong to. Same photograph size, same arithmetic, same bars. Showing the good person on the other side at equal size is what keeps a partisan front door from becoming a campaign tool.

## What keeps it honest

No string on any door compares the parties, characterizes another party, or attributes motive to one. The unit of judgement is a person and a vote. A door explains the same finding to a different audience; it does not produce a different finding.

Two more limits are worth stating plainly, because both would be easy to quietly break:

* **The cross-party column is always shown.** When no challenger from another party has a sourced record yet, the column renders an honest empty state at full width — never a collapsed column, never a shifted layout that hides the absence.
* **The doors do no fundraising, take no donations, and coordinate with no campaign.** The movement endorses people, through a published approval path, and never a party.

The doors are also deliberately narrow. They are built for reading: your seats, the record behind them, and the people who could take those seats. The reasoning tools, your own recorded positions, your value system and the evidence chains all live in the app itself, on this neutral site's side of the line.

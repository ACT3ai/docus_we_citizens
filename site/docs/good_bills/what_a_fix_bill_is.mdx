# What a fix bill is

Three words get used interchangeably in ordinary conversation and mean completely different things
here. Getting them straight makes the whole library legible.

| | Who wrote it | Was it filed | Was it enacted | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem law** | Congress | yes | **yes, it is in force** | Problem Laws |
| **Good law** | a legislator | **yes, somebody filed it** | passed, voted down, or never allowed a vote | Good Laws |
| **Fix bill** | **the movement** | **no** | no | here |

A fix bill is the only one of the three that nobody in government has touched. It exists so that the
answer to *"fine, what would you do instead?"* is a document rather than a slogan.

## The rendering rules that follow from that

* A fix bill page never shows a vote count, because there is no vote.
* It never shows a sponsor, because there is none. If one day somebody files it, that is a different
  record with a different status.
* Its status language is about drafting, never about passage.

## Sections, not paragraphs

Fix bills are written in sections because that is the unit at which they can be criticised, adopted,
or lifted into somebody else's bill. A legislator who wants three of our seven sections should be able
to take three of our seven sections. Nothing about the format is precious.

## The risks block

Every bill carries a `risks` block, and it is not decorative. Where a draft names living people — for
example, naming proposed investigators — the risks block states plainly that naming somebody is a
proposal about a **role**, not a claim about that person, that none of them has consented, and that
the movement is not endorsing any individual by listing them.

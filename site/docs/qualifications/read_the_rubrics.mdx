# Read the rubrics

## Where they are

In the public data repository, one file per area, alongside a roll-call file that lists every area and
carries the weight vector saying how much each contributes.

```bash
git clone https://github.com/ACT3ai/data_we_citizens.git
```

Then read `qualifications/`. No tooling required.

## How to read one

**Start at the leaves, not the summary.** The area name tells you the topic; the leaves tell you what
is actually being measured, and that is where a rubric is either honest or not.

**Read level 5 and level 9 of a leaf together.** The gap between them is the rubric's real content —
what it thinks improvement consists of.

**Look for the mechanism.** A good leaf's high levels describe *how somebody reasons*. A bad leaf's high
levels describe *what somebody concludes*. If you can tell which party wrote it, it is broken.

## How to argue with it

The rubrics are files in a public repository, so disagreement is concrete rather than rhetorical.

**The strongest objection** is to name a leaf and show a political position that cannot reach a 9 on it
without abandoning itself. That is the failure mode the movement most wants reported, and it is the
test the rubrics are supposed to survive.

**The second strongest** is to show a leaf where a high level does not actually indicate competence —
where somebody could score well by being fluent rather than right.

File it through the app, or as a pull request against the file.

## The defamation posture

Rubrics describe **competence in an area**, assessed from what somebody said and did, with the material
cited. They do not characterise people. An assessment is a reading of stated positions against a
published standard — and a low result is a statement about the material we have, which is why the
percent-known figure always travels with it.

## The state of them

Sixteen areas are authored, with 781 leaves. Economics was developed first as the worked example, and
the mechanism discipline was applied outward from it.

They are a first pass by construction — written to be argued with, and improved by people who know an
area better than the person who drafted it.

# Passed, blocked, or buried

## Passed

The easiest record to keep and the least interesting. There is a roll call, the roll call is public,
and the change is in the statute. We record it, we link the vote, we move on.

## Voted down

Also easy: there is a roll call and a margin. What the record adds is *who* — because a citizen
reading their own representative's page should see that the repair bill lost by four votes and theirs
was one of them.

## Never allowed a vote

This is the category the movement exists to surface.

A bill can die without anybody voting against it. It can be held in committee and never scheduled. It
can be denied a rule. It can be left off the calendar until the session ends. It can be swallowed into
a package and stripped in conference. In every one of those cases the outcome is identical to a
defeat, and the record is **blank** — no roll call, no names, nothing for a journalist to cite or a
citizen to be angry about.

Treating a blank as *nothing happened* is precisely the deadlock this movement was built against: the
absence of evidence is manufactured, and then the absence is used to end the conversation.

So we record it as an event:

* **who** held it,
* **at what stage** — committee, rule, calendar, conference,
* **by what mechanism**,
* and **what the source is** for each of those.

## The weight this carries

Blocking a repair from advancing is weighted far more heavily in the [Llama Award](../llama/overview.mdx)
than any single floor vote — by design. A floor vote is one voice among hundreds. Keeping a bill off
the floor is one person deciding for everyone, in a room, without a record.
